# Ricks Clips-inspired visual system for Ninety Sense

This is an original production specification informed by publicly observable Ricks Clips (@RicksClips3000) examples and available analysis. It translates broad visual techniques into a distinct Ninety Sense style. It is not a frame-by-frame recreation.

## The defining visual combination

The key distinction is **not stick figures**. The observed format combines:
1. Stylized 3D scenes for environments, objects, spatial depth, and physical action.
2. Separate flat 2D expressive face/head graphics integrated onto characters.
3. Clear staging so the viewer can understand the action and important prop immediately.
4. Large, selective captions that reinforce the key idea without hiding the action.
5. Narration-led pacing with visible escalation and a comprehensible payoff.

Do not substitute a slideshow of unrelated images, a full-screen talking head, generic stock footage, or a row of simple stick figures.

## Original Ninety Sense implementation

### Scene construction
Build a real 3D-feeling space for each story: bedroom, kitchen, office, street, bus, phone-screen environment, hallway, social gathering, or another setting required by the script. Give the scene foreground, middle-ground, and background. Use a few meaningful props rather than clutter.

### Character system
Create original recurring character designs:
- Consistent body silhouette and proportions.
- Consistent clothing and identifying colors.
- Flat graphic 2D face/head treatment with highly readable expressions.
- Expressions chosen from the exact emotional beat, not random emotion.
- Character placement and eyelines must make the interaction understandable.

Use a character reference sheet before generating a multi-scene sequence. Keep the same character reference and description attached to every scene prompt.

### Shot grammar
Use a mix of wide establishing shots, medium two-character shots, close-ups on expressive faces, inserts of meaningful props, over-shoulder reveals, reaction shots, and return shots that reveal the changed meaning of an earlier image. Every cut must preserve screen direction and spatial logic unless deliberate disorientation is part of the story.

### Story-to-visual mapping
Translate psychological ideas into observable actions:
- Overthinking a text: character types, deletes, checks the phone, sees the typing indicator disappear; show the loop visually.
- Fear of rejection: character approaches a group, hesitates, misreads a neutral reaction, withdraws.
- People-pleasing: character gives away time or an object, then faces the consequence.
- Emotional avoidance: character moves around or hides from a visible reminder rather than confronting it.

These are examples of visual translation, not mandatory repeated plots. Invent a fresh action for the actual script.

### Pacing
Start on the most intriguing action or consequence, not a logo or slow establishing shot. Give the first shot enough time to read, then change visuals with the narration's beats. Use reaction cuts and inserts to make the cause-and-effect chain obvious. Slow down for the realization or emotional payoff. Do not cut so quickly that the viewer cannot read the action or understand the prop. Never manufacture a numeric cut-rate target without measuring actual reference videos and testing audience retention.

### Camera and movement
Prefer controlled camera movement that clarifies action: slow push-in for realization or tension; lateral tracking to follow a character's decision; over-shoulder reveal for a message or object; small parallax for a held composition; locked camera when expression or action should carry the beat. Avoid repetitive zoom-in/zoom-out loops.

### Captions
Hook: short and concrete. Emphasis: one short phrase at a time, timed to the exact spoken idea. Subtitles: accurate spoken words, separate from emphasis captions. Captions must not cover the face, important prop, or explanatory action. Never use generated text inside the image itself; add typography during editing for accuracy.

### Sound
Voiceover stays foregrounded. Music supports the emotional arc without dictating an unearned emotion. Use motivated effects such as phone vibration, footsteps, chair movement, door click, notification, breath, and room tone. Use silence around important realizations when it improves impact. Avoid constant whooshes and impacts.

## Asset generation procedure

For each scene, write a prompt containing:
1. Exact story beat and action.
2. Character reference identity and outfit.
3. Environment and important props.
4. Camera angle, framing, and depth.
5. Expression and body pose.
6. Lighting and visual continuity.
7. Exclusions: no text, logos, watermark, extra characters, duplicate limbs, or unintended duplicate props.

Generate one scene at a time when continuity matters. Inspect the output, correct it, then use it as the reference for the next shot. Do not generate an entire sequence in one prompt and hope continuity survives.

If the available generator cannot preserve a flat 2D face on a 3D character, create the 3D scene and face overlay as separate layers, then composite them in the editor. Do not claim compositing happened unless it was actually performed.

## Final review
- Can a viewer understand the physical action with sound off?
- Is the 3D environment doing real storytelling work?
- Are the 2D expressions clear and consistent?
- Does each scene represent the actual narration?
- Is the important prop/action visible when narration explains it?
- Does the ending resolve or transform the opening image?
- Does the result feel like an original Ninety Sense production rather than a copied Ricks Clips upload?
