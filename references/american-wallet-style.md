# American Wallet — Finance Documentary Visual Direction

## Non-negotiable creative identity

American Wallet is a **finance documentary** channel. Every video must feel like a carefully directed documentary explaining a real financial issue in American life. It is not a generic stock-footage slideshow, a talking-head-only video, a motivational montage, or a sequence of unrelated images of money.

The narration is the story. Visuals must make the financial system, people affected by it, decisions, objects, evidence, and consequences understandable.

## 1. Documentary visual language

Use a deliberate mix of:
- Photorealistic, cinematic documentary-style scenes.
- Realistic American locations and interiors.
- People and character-led moments when the story involves human decisions, experiences, conflict, or consequences.
- Relevant physical objects and close-up inserts.
- Authentic-looking but non-deceptive explanatory graphics.
- Maps, timelines, charts, and document details when they genuinely explain the issue.

Favor naturalistic lighting, believable materials, realistic proportions, thoughtful composition, depth, motivated camera movement, and restrained color grading. Avoid glossy commercial-advertising imagery, fantasy visuals, plastic-looking AI renders, and generic corporate stock-photo compositions.

## 2. Characters are required when the story needs people

Do not make a character-free video by default. Include people whenever the narration describes or implies a human experience, decision, risk, benefit, conflict, or consequence.

Relevant characters may include:
- Older Americans and retirees.
- Couples and families planning or discussing retirement.
- Workers approaching retirement.
- Adult children helping parents with finances.
- Financial advisors, bank employees, employers, healthcare workers, or public officials when relevant to the story.

Show purposeful, believable actions rather than posing for the camera: reviewing a statement, opening a letter, comparing bills, paying at a grocery store, discussing a decision at a kitchen table, checking a bank balance, working a shift, or considering a major purchase.

Use facial expression, body language, gesture, eyelines, and interaction to communicate the emotional stakes. Include close-ups and reaction shots when they add meaning.

### Character continuity

When a person recurs:
- Keep age range, face, hair, clothing, build, and other identifying visual traits consistent.
- Maintain continuity of location, time of day, props, and screen direction within a sequence.
- Use a character reference image or equivalent reference workflow when available.
- Do not silently replace a recurring person with a different-looking generated character.
- Do not invent a real person's identity or imply that a fictional illustrative character is an actual news subject.

Characters are not mandatory in every shot. Use objects, environments, documents, maps, or graphics when those communicate the beat more clearly. The rule is story-led casting, not people in every frame.

## 3. The documentary object library

Select objects from the actual subject of the narration. Examples include:

**Retirement and Social Security:** envelopes and letters, retirement folders, benefit statements, calendars, appointment notes, pension paperwork, savings records, calculators, glasses, household files.

**Everyday cost of living:** grocery receipts, shopping baskets, price labels, utility bills, rent notices, fuel pumps, pharmacy receipts, household appliances, insurance paperwork.

**Banking, debt, and savings:** bank cards, checkbooks, account statements, loan paperwork, mortgage documents, payment notices, calculators, cash, savings jars, computers and phones displaying neutral non-branded interfaces.

**Taxes, policy, and government:** tax paperwork, official public information, public buildings, legislative settings, printed notices, forms and charts—only when relevant and represented accurately.

Objects must be legible enough to communicate the intended idea. Avoid meaningless piles of cash, random coins, floating dollar signs, arbitrary paperwork, and repetitive calculator shots.

Never fabricate a government letter, official seal, financial statement, named institution document, account balance, or numerical evidence. If a document is illustrative, make it generic and do not present it as an authentic official record.

## 4. Scene selection: match the visual to the narration

For every narration beat, choose the strongest visual form:

| Narration beat | Preferred visual treatment |
|---|---|
| A person faces a financial decision | Character performing the decision or discussing it |
| A cost or bill changes | Relevant bill, receipt, price label, or household purchase |
| A retirement concern is introduced | Retiree or couple in a believable daily-life setting |
| A rule, benefit, or policy is explained | Accurate document detail, timeline, map, or clear graphic |
| A number is compared | Simple, sourced chart or clean comparison graphic |
| A consequence is described | Human reaction or observable real-world consequence |
| A place or institution matters | Relevant American location or institution exterior/interior |
| A complex process is explained | Step-by-step visual sequence with objects, people, and explanatory graphics |

Do not illustrate every sentence literally. Group related narration into coherent sequences and vary shot scale: establishing wide shots, medium action shots, close-ups, inserts, over-the-shoulder views, and reaction shots.

Every shot must add information, context, emotion, or progression. Do not use random B-roll merely to fill time.

## 5. Financial evidence and graphics

- Use exact figures, dates, policy details, and claims from the supplied script or verified source material.
- Preserve units, time periods, comparison baselines, and qualifications.
- Charts must accurately represent the underlying data; never invent data to make a scene more dramatic.
- Label illustrative scenarios clearly as examples, not real people or verified cases.
- Do not generate legible fake official documents. Use generic, non-deceptive props for illustrative scenes and reserve exact factual claims for sourced graphics or verified on-screen text.
- If a key claim cannot be substantiated from supplied material, flag it instead of visually presenting it as fact.

## 6. Editing, camera, and sound

Use documentary pacing: allow important evidence and human reactions time to register, then move on when the narration changes beat. Avoid arbitrary rapid cuts and repetitive slow zooms.

Camera movement must have a reason: reveal a document detail, follow an action, establish a location, move from a person to the object they are considering, or emphasize a meaningful change.

Use restrained documentary music and purposeful environmental sound. Keep narration intelligible and dominant. Do not use sensational sound design to imply danger or certainty beyond the evidence.

## 7. Brand system

- Deep Navy: `#14263D`
- Warm Ivory: `#F5F1E8`
- Use these as the channel's primary graphic and text palette.
- Keep graphics clean, restrained, high-contrast, and readable on mobile.
- Preserve the supplied official host reference exactly when the host is requested or appears. Do not replace the host with a generic younger presenter.
- Do not add extra brand colors, decorative gold, random logos, or unrequested subscribe prompts.
- Do not apply Ninety Sense's stylized 3D environment / flat 2D face system.

## 8. Required production workflow

1. Break the voiceover into story beats and identify the human stakes, financial mechanism, evidence, and consequences.
2. Decide where characters are needed. If the narration describes people, their choices, or their lived consequences, plan character-led scenes rather than defaulting to object-only visuals.
3. Build a recurring cast only when the story benefits from continuity; define each recurring character before generating their scenes.
4. Build a varied shot list that deliberately mixes characters, environments, objects, evidence, and graphics.
5. Generate or source each visual using tools actually available. Inspect outputs for realism, continuity, factual integrity, and relevance.
6. Edit to the voiceover, keeping documentary pacing and clear visual progression.
7. Review the complete video for visual variety, meaningful character use, accurate evidence, brand consistency, and absence of generic filler.

## Final quality test

Before delivery, ask:
- Does this unmistakably feel like a finance documentary?
- Are people shown when the story requires human experience or consequences?
- Do recurring characters remain visually consistent?
- Does each object or location have a clear reason to be on screen?
- Are financial figures and documents accurate and non-deceptive?
- Does the video explain the financial story rather than merely decorate the narration?
- Are the Navy and Ivory brand colors respected?
- Is the visual sequence varied, coherent, and watchable for the full runtime?

If any answer is no, correct the edit before calling it finished.
