# American Wallet — Finance Documentary Visual Direction

## Non-negotiable creative identity

American Wallet is a **finance documentary** channel. Every video must feel like a carefully directed documentary explaining a real financial issue in American life. It is not a generic stock-footage slideshow, a talking-head-only video, a motivational montage, or a sequence of unrelated images of money.

The narration is the story. Visuals must make the financial system, people affected by it, decisions, objects, evidence, and consequences understandable.

## 1. Per-project frame format

American Wallet supports **both 16:9 landscape and 9:16 portrait**. The user selects one format for each production; do not assume a permanent channel format.

- For **16:9**, compose as a widescreen documentary: use horizontal environmental context, layered depth, wide-to-close shot progression, and layouts that use the full landscape frame.
- For **9:16**, redesign each composition for a vertical screen: prioritize one clear subject, stack information vertically, simplify charts and document layouts, reposition characters and important objects, and keep titles/subtitles inside portrait-safe areas.
- Generate or compose assets for the selected format from the beginning. Do not simply crop a landscape edit into portrait or stretch a portrait asset into landscape.
- The selected format controls the entire project: scene framing, camera movement, graphics, captions, subtitles, safe areas, and final export.
- The channel's documentary identity, Deep Navy/Warm Ivory foundation, script-led semantic color decisions, and editorial standards remain the same in either format.

## 2. Documentary visual language

Use a deliberate mix of:
- Photorealistic, cinematic documentary-style scenes.
- Realistic American locations and interiors.
- People and character-led moments when the story involves human decisions, experiences, conflict, or consequences.
- Relevant physical objects and close-up inserts.
- Authentic-looking but non-deceptive explanatory graphics.
- Maps, timelines, charts, and document details when they genuinely explain the issue.

Favor naturalistic lighting, believable materials, realistic proportions, thoughtful composition, depth, motivated camera movement, and restrained color grading. Avoid glossy commercial-advertising imagery, fantasy visuals, plastic-looking AI renders, and generic corporate stock-photo compositions.



## 2A. VOX-caliber finance documentary standard — mandatory

**The target is a fully produced, information-rich finance documentary explainer with the visual clarity and editorial discipline associated with high-quality VOX-style documentaries. This means the viewer should understand the financial story by watching, not merely hear narration over decorative pictures.** Treat VOX as a quality and visual-communication reference, not a request to copy its exact branding, templates, wording, or proprietary assets.

### No empty-screen / audio-only coverage

- Never leave a blank page, empty canvas, black screen, unfilled scene, default editor background, or meaningless static frame running under narration.
- Every audible narration beat must have an intentional visual: live-action-style scene, relevant documentary image, archive/news visual, map, chart, timeline, labeled diagram, document close-up, animated object, kinetic typography, or another story-relevant treatment.
- A visual can be quiet or minimal when that is the editorial choice, but it must still communicate something. Empty space is not a substitute for a finished visual.
- Do not stretch one still image across a long passage simply because no other asset was prepared. Use a purposeful sequence of crops, details, overlays, animated annotations, contextual inserts, or a newly created visual sequence. Do not fake motion with meaningless zooms.
- If a required visual cannot be generated or sourced, do not leave a hole. Create a clear explanatory visual using information already present in the supplied script or approved project assets, or report the specific production limitation. Do not invent figures, documents, headlines, or claims.

### Documentary visual toolkit

Select and combine these tools according to the story. Use real visual explanation, not a slideshow:

1. **Real-world documentary imagery:** people, workplaces, homes, banks, stores, government buildings, neighborhoods, and relevant everyday environments. Show observable actions and consequences, not generic posed stock-photo scenes.
2. **Documents and context visuals:** user-supplied or approved headlines, public records, official documents, reports, filings, policy pages, historical photographs, and source excerpts. Preserve their supplied context; do not invent documents or imply an illustrative prop is an authentic record.
3. **Data visualization:** custom charts, comparisons, timelines, annotated figures, trend lines, proportional graphics, and simple calculations. Every number must match the supplied final script or approved project assets. Make the takeaway visually obvious.
4. **Maps and systems:** geographic maps, flow diagrams, institutional relationships, money flows, process diagrams, and cause-and-effect sequences when they explain how a financial system works.
5. **Motion graphics and visual annotation:** animated labels, callouts, highlights, arrows, underlines, counters, tracked figures, document focus, transitions between scales, and progressive reveals. Motion must clarify the point, not decorate it.
6. **Human stories:** carefully directed character scenes and reactions when the story involves people, choices, vulnerability, conflict, or consequences. Maintain continuity when characters recur.
7. **Typography as explanation:** concise on-screen phrases, key figures, dates, definitions, and comparisons. Use typography to emphasize the argument or make complex information legible, not to repeat every spoken sentence.

### Editorial and pacing requirements

- Build an editorial visual argument: establish the real-world problem, show the mechanism, reveal evidence, explain who benefits or pays, and make the consequences understandable. Adapt this structure to the script; do not force a formula.
- Give important points a visual explanation. When narration says a figure, policy, institution, trend, or comparison matters, show the supplied number, mechanism, or consequence in a form the viewer can inspect.
- Use visual changes when the information changes. Vary wide context, medium action, close detail, graphic explanation, and evidence inserts. Avoid both frantic cutting and long stretches of visual inactivity.
- Use on-screen text selectively and legibly. Highlight the exact key phrase or figure at the moment it is spoken. Do not cover the frame with paragraphs.
- Keep graphics editorially clean: strong hierarchy, restrained motion, consistent typography, readable labels, and clear units. Use the Deep Navy and Warm Ivory brand palette. Adapt graphic layout to the selected aspect ratio.
- Make transitions explain relationships: move from person to bill, bill to price trend, price trend to policy, or institution to consequence. Avoid unrelated transition effects.
- Every shot must perform a job: establish, humanize, prove, compare, explain, reveal, or connect. Remove any shot that does none of these.

### Supplied-script fidelity

This production skill is responsible for visual storytelling and video editing, not independent financial fact-checking. Treat the supplied final script and user-provided project assets as the content source for the edit. Do not add independent financial research, fact verification, or a separate evidence-tracking workflow. Do not invent extra figures, quotes, policy details, headlines, or official documents. If a value or detail is not supplied in the script or approved assets, leave it out or use a clearly generic illustrative visual.

### Mandatory pre-export visual coverage audit

Review the full timeline from first frame to last frame, with the voiceover playing. Confirm:
- No blank, black, empty, missing-media, or default-background frames.
- No narration passage is left with no meaningful visual explanation.
- No long static hold exists merely because the edit lacks assets.
- Every important financial claim, figure, institution, and consequence receives appropriate visual support.
- Charts, maps, labels, and document details are readable at normal YouTube viewing size.
- Visuals are specific to this script and are not interchangeable generic finance filler.

If any check fails, fix the timeline before export. A video with empty screens or voiceover over unprepared placeholders is not a completed American Wallet video.

## 2B. Collage-explainer treatment — style reference, original work only

Use for concept-led graphics, title cards, and key explanatory visuals where a layered collage explains the story better than a literal photo. It complements the documentary language above; it does not replace it.

- **Technique:** layered torn-paper and cutout collage, grain and halftone texture, tape and paper-clip details.
- **Composition:** one strong central subject (an object, building, symbol, or silhouette) with smaller supporting pieces around it. Use overlap for depth. Use a split composition when the story has two sides or a clear contrast.
- **Information:** put key figures, dates, rules, and short quotes on paper notes or tags. Use only details supplied in the final script or approved project assets, a few per frame, readable at the selected format.
- **Palette:** Deep Navy and Warm Ivory remain the brand foundation. For data or financial meaning, Claude may add restrained semantic colors only when the script beat requires them: muted green for a clearly positive outcome (gains, growth, increases in benefits, improving balances) and muted red for a clearly negative outcome (losses, cuts, debt increases, declining balances, risk). These are informational signals, not decorative brand colors. Neutral explanatory content stays navy/ivory or subdued neutral tones. Never use red/green merely to make a frame more dramatic, and never imply a value is good or bad without support from the narration.
- **Originality:** any reference images supplied for this style only define the general feel. Never reproduce a specific composition, layout, artwork, poster, or thumbnail. No third-party logos or channel branding.
- Section 2A still applies in full: no empty frames and no fabricated documents or numbers.

## 3. Characters are required when the story needs people

Do not make a character-free video by default. Include people whenever the narration describes or implies a human experience, decision, risk, benefit, conflict, or consequence.

Relevant characters may include:
- Older Americans and retirees.
- Couples and families planning or discussing retirement.
- Workers approaching retirement.
- Adult children helping parents with finances.
- Financial advisors, bank employees, employers, healthcare workers, or public officials when relevant to the story.

Show purposeful, believable actions rather than posing for the camera: reviewing a statement, opening a letter, comparing bills, paying at a grocery store, discussing a decision at a kitchen table, checking a bank balance, working a shift, or considering a major purchase.

Use facial expression, body language, gesture, eyelines, and interaction to communicate the emotional stakes. Include close-ups and reaction shots when they add meaning.

### Character continuity

When a person recurs:
- Keep age range, face, hair, clothing, build, and other identifying visual traits consistent.
- Maintain continuity of location, time of day, props, and screen direction within a sequence.
- Use a character reference image or equivalent reference workflow when available.
- Do not silently replace a recurring person with a different-looking generated character.
- Do not invent a real person's identity or imply that a fictional illustrative character is an actual news subject.

Characters are not mandatory in every shot. Use objects, environments, documents, maps, or graphics when those communicate the beat more clearly. The rule is story-led casting, not people in every frame.

## 4. The documentary object library

Select objects from the actual subject of the narration. Examples include:

**Retirement and Social Security:** envelopes and letters, retirement folders, benefit statements, calendars, appointment notes, pension paperwork, savings records, calculators, glasses, household files.

**Everyday cost of living:** grocery receipts, shopping baskets, price labels, utility bills, rent notices, fuel pumps, pharmacy receipts, household appliances, insurance paperwork.

**Banking, debt, and savings:** bank cards, checkbooks, account statements, loan paperwork, mortgage documents, payment notices, calculators, cash, savings jars, computers and phones displaying neutral non-branded interfaces.

**Taxes, policy, and government:** tax paperwork, official public information, public buildings, legislative settings, printed notices, forms and charts—only when relevant and represented accurately.

Objects must be legible enough to communicate the intended idea. Avoid meaningless piles of cash, random coins, floating dollar signs, arbitrary paperwork, and repetitive calculator shots.

Never fabricate a government letter, official seal, financial statement, named institution document, account balance, or numerical evidence. If a document is illustrative, make it generic and do not present it as an authentic official record.

## 5. Scene selection: match the visual to the narration

For every narration beat, choose the strongest visual form:

| Narration beat | Preferred visual treatment |
|---|---|
| A person faces a financial decision | Character performing the decision or discussing it |
| A cost or bill changes | Relevant bill, receipt, price label, or household purchase |
| A retirement concern is introduced | Retiree or couple in a believable daily-life setting |
| A rule, benefit, or policy is explained | Accurate document detail, timeline, map, or clear graphic |
| A number is compared | Simple chart based on supplied values or clean comparison graphic |
| A consequence is described | Human reaction or observable real-world consequence |
| A place or institution matters | Relevant American location or institution exterior/interior |
| A complex process is explained | Step-by-step visual sequence with objects, people, and explanatory graphics |

Do not illustrate every sentence literally. Group related narration into coherent sequences and vary shot scale: establishing wide shots, medium action shots, close-ups, inserts, over-the-shoulder views, and reaction shots.

Every shot must add information, context, emotion, or progression. Do not use random B-roll merely to fill time.

## 6. Financial graphics and documents

- Use the figures, dates, policy details, names, and claims exactly as supplied in the final script or approved project assets.
- Preserve units, time periods, comparison baselines, and qualifications that appear in the supplied material.
- Charts must accurately visualize supplied values; never invent data to make a scene more dramatic.
- Label hypothetical or illustrative scenarios as examples when the script presents them that way.
- Do not fabricate official documents, government letters, financial statements, account balances, seals, logos, or quotations. Use generic non-deceptive props for illustrative scenes.
- Do not add a source-research, financial fact-checking, or evidence-tracking stage to the video workflow.

## 7. Documentary sound design — mandatory

Load and follow `references/sound-design-workflow.md` for the complete sound map, music editing, ambience, foley, transitions, mixing, source discipline, and final audio QC. The direction below defines the American Wallet-specific sonic identity.

**Sound design is a required part of every American Wallet video. Do not deliver a voiceover-only soundtrack.** The audio must make the documentary world feel real, guide attention, and sustain retention without becoming sensational.

Build a layered soundtrack for every scene:

- **Music bed:** use cinematic documentary scoring that follows the specific financial story. Create curiosity and restrained tension for the setup, measured momentum for explanations, emotional weight for human consequences, and a clear release/payoff. Evolve the music across sections; do not loop one generic track unchanged for the entire video. Use subtle transitions, builds, drops, and restrained accents at story turns.
- **Environmental ambience:** give each location an appropriate sound bed—quiet home room tone, kitchen ambience, office hum, bank or store atmosphere, street traffic, public-space murmur, weather, or other credible environmental sound. Maintain continuity across shots in the same place.
- **Specific action SFX:** deliberately add scene-matched effects whenever the viewer sees an action or important object: paper and envelope handling, letter opening, page turns, calculator keys, pen marks, keyboard typing, phone taps/notifications, footsteps, chair movement, grocery scanner, receipt printing, cash/register sounds, door movement, car ambience, and other relevant details. Use a broad, story-specific vocabulary rather than repeating the same few effects.
- **Documentary emphasis and transitions:** subtle low impacts, restrained risers, swells, or sound bridges can underline a key figure, reveal, change of location, or consequence. Use only when motivated by the story; avoid trailer-style overdramatization.
- **Voiceover:** always remains clear and forward. Duck music and competing ambience/SFX under speech; let selected effects emerge in narration gaps. Avoid clipping, harsh peaks, and muddy low frequencies.

Create a beat-by-beat sound map with the shot plan. Specify each layer, source/action, timing, and intended emotional or informational purpose. Do not use random whooshes as a substitute for sound design. Brief intentional silence is permitted only as a deliberate dramatic choice, not because the soundtrack was forgotten.

Use documentary pacing: allow important evidence and human reactions to register, then move on when the narration changes beat. Camera movement must reveal a detail, follow an action, establish a location, move from a person to the object they consider, or emphasize a meaningful change. Sync music transitions and SFX to the actual edit.

## 8. Brand system

### Color
- Deep Navy: `#14263D`
- Warm Ivory: `#F5F1E8`
- Use these as the channel's primary graphic and text palette.
- Do not add gold, red, mustard, pink, or other decorative brand colors.

### Typography system — mandatory
Use a consistent two-family editorial system across every American Wallet production. Do not choose random fonts scene by scene.

- **Display headlines and major financial figures: Barlow Condensed ExtraBold (800).** Use for short editorial headlines, section titles, strong key phrases, and the most important numbers. It should feel authoritative and legible, not like a tabloid or sports graphic.
- **Supporting copy, chart labels, captions, subtitles, and explanatory text: Inter.** Use regular (400) for body/supporting copy, medium (500–600) for labels, and bold (700) only for selective emphasis.
- **Thumbnail headline: Barlow Condensed ExtraBold (800), uppercase.** Use Warm Ivory text directly on the established Deep Navy background. Keep the existing text-only thumbnail identity: no faces or humans, no boxes, banners, outlines, underlines, shadows, decorative lines, or extra colors.
- **Numbers:** use Barlow Condensed ExtraBold for the primary number and Inter for its unit, context, date, or qualifier. Keep the full meaning and units visible; never make a number dramatic at the expense of clarity.
- **Kinetic typography:** reveal a key phrase or figure when the narration reaches it. Use restrained word-by-word, type-on, tracking, scale, or position changes only when they improve comprehension or emphasis. Do not animate every word, make all text oversized, or copy another creator's exact motion templates.
- **Approved high-impact treatments:** (1) a large Barlow Condensed financial figure with its unit and context in Inter; (2) a clean side-by-side comparison with aligned labels and values; (3) a restrained document/statistic callout using Warm Ivory on Deep Navy; (4) a short section kicker above a strong headline. Choose only the treatment that explains the current beat. No decorative boxes, gold accents, fake ticker graphics, or clutter.
- **Hierarchy:** one dominant message per frame; secondary explanation clearly smaller; tertiary labels smallest but still readable at the selected format. Keep line lengths short and avoid dense paragraphs.
- **Consistency:** preserve the same font roles, weights, and visual hierarchy throughout the video, thumbnails, charts, and recurring graphic elements. Adapt size and layout for 16:9 versus 9:16 without changing the font system.
- **Font availability:** use the named font files when available in the editing environment. If a required family is unavailable, use a metrically and stylistically close sans-serif fallback consistently for the entire project and disclose the substitution. Do not silently mix unrelated typefaces or claim the named font was used if it was not.

Keep graphics clean, restrained, high-contrast, and readable on mobile. Preserve the supplied official host reference exactly when the host is requested or appears. Do not replace the host with a generic younger presenter. Do not apply Ninety Sense's stylized 3D environment / flat 2D face system.

### Script-led semantic color — mandatory

Deep Navy (`#14263D`) and Warm Ivory (`#F5F1E8`) remain the permanent American Wallet brand foundation. They define the documentary identity, backgrounds, primary typography, and neutral explanatory graphics. **They are not a restriction that forces every data visualization to be navy and ivory only.**

Claude must read the actual script beat and choose colors according to the meaning being communicated:

- **Muted green:** only for a clearly positive financial direction or outcome, such as gains, growth, an increase in benefits, improving savings, or a rising balance when that rise is beneficial in context.
- **Muted red:** only for a clearly negative direction or outcome, such as losses, cuts, rising debt, declining balances, reduced benefits, financial risk, or a harmful cost increase.
- **Neutral navy, ivory, slate, and restrained grays:** for explanations, historical context, institutions, policy mechanics, neutral comparisons, and values whose positive/negative meaning is not established.
- **Other colors:** use only when a real documentary object, map, category distinction, or accessibility need requires it. Keep the palette restrained and explainable; never add color just for decoration.

The script's context determines meaning. An increase is not automatically good (debt, costs, taxes, and risk can rise); a decrease is not automatically bad (debt or expenses can fall). Do not color-code a number until Claude understands what the number represents and whether the change benefits or harms the people in the story.

Use muted, documentary-appropriate shades with strong contrast and consistent meaning throughout a video. Keep red and green distinguishable with labels, symbols, or direction cues; never rely on color alone. Do not use saturated neon green/red, flashing colors, or stock trading-dashboard styling.

For each chart, comparison, callout, or collage graphic, Claude must decide whether semantic color improves comprehension. If it does not, stay with the core navy/ivory identity. Color must explain the story, never overpower it.

## 9. Required production workflow

1. Read the final script and listen to the supplied voiceover. Use the selected project format from the shared output contract.
2. Interpret the story and invent an original documentary treatment for this specific video.
3. Break the voiceover into meaningful timed beats. Plan the visual purpose, narration phrase, scene action, framing, movement, on-screen text, and sound layers for each beat.
4. Decide where characters, environments, objects, documents, charts, maps, timelines, diagrams, or collage graphics best communicate the narration.
5. Identify only the images the story genuinely needs. Follow `references/image-prompt-workflow.md` to create detailed, ready-to-paste prompts for each required image, then pause for the user's generated image uploads. Inspect the uploaded images and maintain character, prop, and location continuity. Do not require a connected image-generation API.
6. Organize assets by scene and give them clear names tied to the storyboard. Keep the original voiceover filename available for the final export name.
7. Build the picture edit, animation, captions, subtitles, music, ambience, and story-specific sound effects around the actual voiceover.
8. Generate a preview and inspect the full timeline. Fix missing visuals, weak pacing, unreadable graphics, continuity problems, mistimed captions, and audio issues.
9. Export one MP4 in the selected aspect ratio, using the exact voiceover-derived filename required by `references/output-contract.md`.
10. Verify that the MP4 opens, matches the selected aspect ratio, has the correct duration and filename, and contains the completed picture and sound mix.

## Final quality test

Before delivery, ask:
- Does the final video match the format selected for this production (16:9 or 9:16)?
- Does this unmistakably feel like a finance documentary?
- Are people shown when the story requires human experience or consequences?
- Do recurring characters remain visually consistent?
- Does each object or location have a clear reason to be on screen?
- Are figures, labels, and documents represented faithfully from the supplied script and approved assets, without fabricated details?
- Does the video explain the financial story rather than merely decorate the narration?
- Are Deep Navy and Warm Ivory used as the brand foundation, with red/green applied only when the script's financial meaning justifies them?
- Are semantic colors contextually correct (for example, a rise in debt is not colored as a positive gain), restrained, consistent, and never used as decoration?
- Is the visual sequence varied, coherent, and watchable for the full runtime?
- Is there an evolving music bed, appropriate ambience, and varied story-specific SFX—not just voiceover?
- Are sound effects synced to visible actions, and are music/SFX mixed below narration when speech is present?

If any answer is no, correct the edit before calling it finished.
